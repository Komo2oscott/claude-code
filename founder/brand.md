## 1. Positioning in one breath

**The founder's model:** take a commodity and turn it into culture through design. The founder points to Liquid Death's designed cans and music roots as the comparison. DIRTY's version is rooted in **the art world**, and its commodity is **salad greens**.

**What DIRTY stands for:** food that's still alive, for people who'd rather be outside.

- **Product truth:** organic, grown in USA fields, factory wash skipped, lasts longer. Pilot buyers bought for shelf life and no chemicals.
- **Cultural truth:** the cure for screen time. Get outside, get your hands dirty, let your hair down. The Instagram account already runs on this ("You're not tired, you're understimulated", "What I did instead of doomscrolling").
- **Enemy:** the factory. Bagged-salad wash tanks and food that's processed to be convenient and comes out bland. DIRTYLAND already names the hazards: "Bagged Salad Swamp", "Chemical Wash Tank", "Skip the Factory".

**Competitor reaction:** the founder reports that organic-greens competitors, organic girl among them, "noticed us immediately and were pissed." That is a useful signal that DIRTY is disruptive. It is not yet evidence the board can score. See section 7 for how to turn it into some.

## 3. Promise, taglines, voice

**The promise:** *Organic greens with the factory wash skipped, so they taste like the field and stay fresh longer.*

**Taglines (pick one per job):**
1. **"Skip the factory."** For the pack and the shelf. It states the product truth and works in a lunchbox.
2. **"Get dirty."** For social, merch and events. It's the cultural invitation, so it can lead the lifestyle content.
3. **"Still alive."** For the art campaigns. It's short enough for a gallery wall.

*"Fresh as f\*ck" stays where it works, on merch and adult social. **Keep it off the pack.*** The back-to-school lunchbox post shows parents are already a real audience. Profanity on a clamshell also narrows the list of grocers who will stock you.

**Voice: three adjectives**

| | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| **Irreverent** | Poke fun at the factory, the wash tank, the bagged-salad swamp | Mock the shopper, or joke about food safety. After a cyclospora scare, "dirty" must always read as *alive*, never *unsafe* |
| **Artful** | Treat every pack, poster and post as a piece of work, with a named artist where you can | Use art-world jargon nobody outside a gallery speaks |
| **Outdoorsy-playful** | Invite people to wander, play, touch grass, make a mess | Preach wellness or guilt people about screens |

**Before → after** (the site's about-us line):
- Before: *"DIRTY is more than a bag of greens. It's a movement."*
- After: *"Somewhere between the field and your plate, food stopped being alive. We put the dirt back. You bring the water."*

## 4. The art-world strategy, made specific

This is how the founder's art-world model turns into marketing that costs little, because **the pack itself becomes the media**:

1. **DIRTY Editions: an artist-designed label each quarter.** One clamshell sleeve or label per character each season, made by a named artist and paid a fee or royalty. Each edition is a reason to post, a reason for press and a reason to collect. A QR code on the pack links to the artist's page.
2. **The characters are the art collection.** Sir Leafs-A-Lot, Rosie, Kali-fornia and Astro work like the cast of a comic. Each gets a short story, and each artist reimagines them. "Find Astro" and DIRTYLAND prove the format works.
3. **The Propaganda posters are prints.** Sell them as limited, numbered prints. The poster set is already in the store at $15. It turns merch into art, and the margin into marketing money.
4. **Demos become openings.** Turn the $60 in-store demo into a small art moment: the edition artist on site, a print giveaway with purchase. Same cost, more to post.
5. **Gallery and outdoor pop-ups.** Taste the greens at an art walk or an outdoor event, which links the art thread to the "get outside" thread. Pick events by cost per pack sold, the same way you judge demos.

*Measure each one* by packs sold, email signups, and repeat purchase in the stores nearby. A campaign that builds buzz without repeat buyers is the expensive mistake the board warned about.

## 5. The look: a brief

**Colour**, taken from the live site and the Instagram grid. Contrast was computed with the WCAG formula.

| Role | Hex | Job | Text contrast |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DIRTY Lime** | `#D0FE1D` | The signal: shelf stopper, highlights, the hat | Black text **16.1:1** ✓ AAA. **White text 1.2:1 ✗, never use it** |
| **Ink** | `#111111` | Wordmark, type, the "gallery wall" | Lime text **16.1:1** ✓ |
| **Field Green** | `#1F7A3A` | Secondary: hats, merch, outdoor | White **5.4:1** ✓ AA. Lime **4.6:1** ✓ AA (normal text, barely) |
| *Accent:* Harvest Orange | `#F26B1D` | Seasonal campaigns only ("Happy Fall") | Black **6.2:1** ✓. White **3.0:1**: large text only |
| *Ground:* Paper | `#F5F1E8` | Packaging and print backgrounds, the art-paper feel | Black **16.8:1** ✓. Field Green **4.8:1** ✓ |

**Type**, as the site already uses:
- **Display: Dela Gothic One**, free on Google Fonts under the SIL Open Font License. It's heavy and poster-like, which suits the Propaganda look.
- **Text: Instrument Sans**, free on Google Fonts under the OFL. Clean and readable for labels and the site.
- Hand-drawn or graffiti lettering ("Healthy soil, healthy food, healthy people") should be commissioned art with the rights in writing, not a downloaded font.

**Logo brief:** keep the DIRTY wordmark with "IS BETTER" under it.
- **It must work at 32px.** At that size "IS BETTER" disappears, so make a **D monogram on a soil-texture circle** (the Instagram avatar already does this) for icons, favicons and lids.
- **It must work on:** the clamshell lid, the shelf strip, a hat front, a poster corner and a TikTok watermark.
- **Avoid:** stacking more taglines into the mark, and any version in lime on white.

The character art is the second identity layer and sits *beside* the wordmark, never inside it. I can generate concepts for the D monogram or an Editions label if you have an image tool connected; anything I made would be a concept, not finished art.

## 6. The first five touchpoints

1. **The shelf and the pack.** The lid shows the wordmark plus one character. On the front: "Organic · Factory wash skipped · Lasts longer". Also on the front, not hidden on the back: "Wash me first: soak 5 min, rinse, shake." The safety line is part of the brand's confidence.
2. **The demo table.** A lime tablecloth, the edition artist or a character standee, and a print with purchase. A sign reads "Taste what the factory washed out."
3. **The order confirmation (merch store).** *"You're officially DIRTY. Your order's packed. Now go outside while you wait."* Add a link to this week's "Ways to get DIRTY in LA".
4. **The first post a new follower sees:** a pinned three-post intro. (1) What DIRTY is and why the wash is skipped. (2) Meet the characters / DIRTYLAND. (3) Where to buy: Gelson's LA + Santa Barbara.
5. **The reply to the first complaint**, say "I found grit". *"That's the field saying hi, and it shouldn't have made it to your plate. Give it the 5-minute soak and a good rinse in the bag. If it's still not right, DM us your store and we'll make it right."* Then replace or refund. Never let the joke replace the fix.

## 7. What would get the board to 7–10

The board scored 4–5 because it is grading **evidence**, not vision. All three already accept the brand as the asset. What holds the score down is proof. Here is what moves each lens, in order of impact:

| # | Evidence | Moves | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Your break-even model, line by line, plus a co-packer quote.** It should show the cost per container falling to break-even at 30–40 stores and profit at 50–60 | All three, by the most | This is the unexplained ~$5.40–$7.40. If the cost per container provably falls with volume, the main thing that would kill it is gone |
| 2 | **Velocity and repeat:** units per store per week, repeat rate, and the weeks after the cyclospora news faded | All three | Proves demand is real and not borrowed from a scare. It also proves the 60-store math, which assumes pilot velocity |
| 3 | **Retailer pull in writing:** Gelson's adding Santa Barbara (already happened), a letter of intent for more Gelson's stores, or a second premium chain interested | Monopoly, Offers | Retailers asking for more is the strongest proof of distribution a small brand can show |
| 4 | **Demo payback:** the share of demo buyers who buy again | Offers, Product | Turns "we need marketing $$" into "every $1 of demo spend returns $X". Example: at a $1.00 profit per container, a $2.00 demo pays back once a buyer makes 2 more purchases |
| 5 | **Brand pull, measured:** IG engagement rate, merch revenue and margin, email list size, creator-program results, press mentions, and the **competitor reaction documented** (what organic girl or others actually did) | Monopoly, Offers | Converts "they were pissed" into proof the brand has pull. Liquid Death-style brands are funded on numbers like these |
| 6 | **The moat on paper:** trademark filings (classes 31 and 25 at least), an exclusive or contracted grower agreement, and a lab-measured shelf-life claim | Monopoly | Answers "what stops a big grower or a store's own label from copying you next season?" |
| 7 | **A capital plan the board can say yes to:** split the ask by job. Production through co-packer terms or purchase-order financing (debt). Marketing through equity or a smaller raise, released in tranches tied to milestones: say a first tranche to 30 stores at break-even, then the next to 60 | All three | The failed $1M raise asked equity to fund everything. Splitting it shrinks the equity ask and ties marketing money to proven payback |

**Honest read:** items 1, 2 and 4 are mostly in the numbers you're pulling now. If they show what your model says (break-even at 30–40 stores, repeat buying holding after the scare, demos paying back), I would expect the scores to move substantially. I can't promise a 7–10 before the board sees them. Items 3, 5 and 6 are cheap to gather and are what push a "fund if" toward a "fund".
