# Monopoly lens (round 2)
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF
Recommended path: hybrid. Stay in greens, the only market DIRTY can own, but fix the unit cost in a narrow LA premium niche before buying scale. Shelf-stable puts the brand in a crowded market it has no edge in.

## What is strong
- **The secret held up in the pilot.** 7 Gelson's stores for 11 weeks, with strong sales and repeat buying, and the reasons buyers gave (longer shelf life, no chemicals) are the contrarian belief itself. Buyers paid $6.99 for it.
- **Timing showed up.** A cyclospora scare tied to a large bagged-salad producer gave "why now" a concrete answer: trust in industrial washing is fragile, and DIRTY is the category built on that doubt.
- **A small market it already leads.** Unwashed greens at a premium LA grocer is a category of one. That is the right starting shape.
- **Distribution exists.** A retailer stocked it, reordered for 11 weeks and is on record as a supporter.

## What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Each sale loses money.** DIRTY nets about $4.54 a container ($6.99 less 35%) against an all-in cost of $6.50 to $8.50. Breaking even needs a cut in unit cost of about 30% to 47%. A monopoly that loses money on every unit is not a business yet, and growing it only makes the losses bigger.
2. **Option A fights the incumbents on scale, their ground.** Financing machinery to chase unit cost puts DIRTY into the acres-and-throughput race that large growers win. The pilot also told every incumbent that "unwashed" sells. A big grower or a private-label buyer can launch an unwashed line next season at a lower cost. DIRTY has no proprietary process, exclusive supply or protected name in view to stop that.
3. **Option B throws away the only secret.** In salsa or prepared meals, "DIRTY" no longer means anything a buyer can check, because the shelf-life and no-chlorine claims do not carry over. The brand would enter a crowded, low-margin category with nothing better about it, the exact market the Monopoly lens says to avoid. The brand is valuable because of what it stands for in greens.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- What are the pilot's unit volumes and repeat rate per store per week? (/founder-cfo)
- Break down the $6.50 to $8.50: growing, packing labour, packaging, mesh bag, freight, slotting. Which lines does machinery cut, and which shrink only with volume? (/founder-ops, /founder-cfo)
- Why does 60 stores turn a profit, at what unit cost, and what does it assume? 60 stores is about 8.6 times the pilot. (/founder-cfo)
- Can the price go up? Would the buyers who came for shelf life and no chemicals pay $7.99 or more? (/founder-pricing, /founder-consumer)
- How many days longer does DIRTY last than pre-washed greens, measured? That number is the edge to defend. (/founder-ops)
- Who could launch an unwashed line fastest, and what stops them? (/founder-competitors)
- What are the current cash and runway?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Positive unit economics before expansion.** Show a path to a cost per container below $4.54 (or a higher price) at roughly the current store count, through pricing, a co-packer, or leased or shared equipment rather than buying machinery.
2. **Own the niche densely first.** Win more stores within premium LA grocers, with measured repeat buying, before chasing 60 stores across several banners.
3. **Build durability.** Exclusive or contracted grower supply, a trademark on the DIRTY category language, and a measured shelf-life claim on pack.
4. **No shelf-stable pivot.** A shelf-stable line is acceptable only later, as an extension once the greens business stands on its own, never as a replacement for it.
