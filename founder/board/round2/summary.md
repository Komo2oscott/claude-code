# Board summary: DIRTY is Better (round 2)

**Vote: 3 FUND IF (Offers 5, Monopoly 4, Product 5). Average score: 4.7 / 10, up from 4.3 in round 1.**

**The decision: none of the three backs the shelf-stable pivot (Option B). All three say stay in greens and fix the cost per container before adding stores.** Product calls this "A, narrowed". Offers and Monopoly call it a hybrid: a price or cost fix in the current stores first, then financing for machinery only if the numbers show that machinery is what closes the gap.

The memos are in `founder/board/` (offers.md, monopoly.md, product.md). Round 1 is in `founder/board/round1/`.

## What changed since round 1

The pilot answered round 1's biggest worries. Buyers paid $6.99, came back, and said they bought for the product's own promises: longer shelf life and no chemicals. The wash step did not drive them away. All three members raised their view of the demand. What replaced those worries is the unit economics.

## Risks more than one member raised

1. **Every container loses money, and more stores multiply the loss (all three).** DIRTY nets about $4.54 a container ($6.99 less the store's 35%) against an all-in cost of about $6.50–$8.50. That is a loss of about $1.96–$3.96 a unit. Breaking even at today's price needs a 30–47% cut in cost. At today's cost, breaking even needs a retail price of about $10.00 (low cost estimate) to $13.08 (high) with the same 35% store share. The 60-store plan comes before the cost fix, so it scales the loss. Nobody has yet shown which parts of the cost machinery removes.
2. **Option B throws away the one thing that sold (all three).** Pilot buyers bought for shelf life and no chemicals. Neither reason applies to salsa or a prepared meal. B keeps the name but drops every reason buyers gave, and enters a crowded category with no pilot, no proof and no edge. Product adds that the brand's own comparison table mocks the kind of processed, shelf-sitting food B would sell.
3. **Demand may have been borrowed from a scare (Offers, Product; Monopoly sees the same fact as a "why now").** The strongest sales came during a cyclospora scare at a large bagged-salad producer. Repeat buying after the news fades is the test.
4. **The pilot told incumbents that "unwashed" sells (Monopoly).** Option A on its own fights large growers on scale, which is their ground. Durability (supply contracts, trademark, a measured shelf-life claim) matters more now.

## Where they disagreed

- **The machinery.** Product would finance machinery once a cost breakdown proves it closes the gap. Monopoly prefers to avoid buying it at all, using a co-packer or leased or shared equipment so DIRTY doesn't enter a scale race. Offers wants a price test before any spend on equipment.
- **Price versus cost.** Offers leads with raising the price ($8.99–$9.99 test). Product leads with cutting cost (the mesh bag in every container, cutting to the best one or two products). Monopoly accepts either, as long as the cost per container falls below $4.54.
- **The scare.** Offers and Product treat it as a risk that demand was borrowed. Monopoly treats it as proof that trust in industrial washing is fragile, the opening DIRTY exists to fill. Both readings lead to the same test: repeat rate after the headlines fade.
- **Where to grow.** Monopoly wants more stores within premium LA grocers before chasing 60 stores across several banners. The others set no geographic limit, only that cost is fixed first.

## The strongest version the board can see

This differs from both options as pitched. The board would not pivot and would not raise money just to buy machinery. It sees the greens business made profitable per container while staying small. That means one or two hero products in the 7 Gelson's stores, possibly a few more premium LA stores nearby, and a price tested toward $8.99–$9.99. The pack would carry a measured "lasts X days longer" claim and a freshness guarantee. The bag would be sold once, with refills after it. The other change would be moving packing to a co-packer, leased line or financed equipment, whichever the cost breakdown shows actually closes the gap. Once each container makes money, growth to 60 stores can be paid for with debt, not equity, because the numbers then pay it back. The brand is the main asset, as the founder says, but the board's reading is that it is valuable *because* of the fresh, unwashed leaf. A salsa or prepared meal could carry the name later as an extension, after the greens have proven it.
