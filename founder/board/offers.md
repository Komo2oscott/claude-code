Recommended path: brand-led, greens first. Make each greens container earn money (co-packer plus a price test), spend on demos only where they pay back through repeat buys, and test whether people buy DIRTY for the brand itself with cheap tests before launching any second product.

## What changed my mind (and what didn't)
- **The arithmetic checks out.** $225 / 200 = $1.13 packing; minus 70% = $0.34, saving $0.79. Net $4.54 ($6.99 x 0.65) against $6.50-$8.50 leaves a loss of $1.96-$3.96, or $1.17-$3.17 after the saving. Break-even still needs a 30-47% cost cut, and packing covers only 20-40% of that gap.
- **Changed:** a co-packer is better than buying machines. It turns a fixed cost into a per-unit cost and needs no equity.
- **Changed:** marketing is a real need, and demos are a good offer tool. At $2.00 a pack they are cheap and measurable. But today a demo-sold pack loses $3.96-$5.96 on the first sale. Marketing makes the losses bigger until each container earns money.
- **Changed, partly:** the brand can be the asset that spans products. Shelf-stable is no longer an automatic no.
- **Not changed:** pilot buyers said they bought for shelf life and no chemicals. Those are product reasons. Nothing yet shows people buying the brand itself (merch sales, social following and engagement are all UNKNOWN).

## The brand-led path: best version and biggest risk
**Best version:** "DIRTY" stands for food that is less processed, less handled and more alive, sold with fun and characters. Every product carries a *true product claim* that matches the name (no chemical wash, short ingredient list), so the brand adds to a real benefit rather than replacing one. Greens stay the proof that the promise is true. The characters make the product easier to learn about and to remember. Merch and the city tours are cheap tests of whether people want the brand for its own sake.
**Biggest risk:** a lifestyle brand sells identity, not relief from a pain. It needs reach that is expensive to buy, bought here while losing money on every unit. A salsa called DIRTY without a dirty-specific benefit is just another salsa with fun packaging.

## What would kill it (top 3)
1. Each container still loses money after the co-packer and price changes, and the remaining ~$5.40-$7.40 of cost never falls. (/founder-cfo, /founder-ops)
2. Demo buyers do not come back, so $2.00 buys one losing sale. (/founder-consumer)
3. The brand pulls no one on its own: merch and social stay flat, and extensions launch with no edge. (/founder-brand, /founder-competitors)

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Line by line, what makes up the $6.50-$8.50, and which of those costs fall with a co-packer or with more stores? (/founder-cfo)
- What does a co-packer quote per unit, with what minimum order and payment terms? (/founder-ops)
- What share of demo buyers buy again within 30 days? (/founder-consumer)
- What do sales do at $8.99 or $9.99? (/founder-pricing)
- What are merch revenue and margin, social reach, and current cash? (/founder-marketing, /founder-cfo)

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. A cost model with a co-packer quote that shows money earned per container at a tested price, before any equipment or debt. (/founder-cfo, /founder-pricing)
2. Split the money roughly in this order: unit cost first, then demos, but only in stores where demo buyers come back enough to pay back the $2.00. Social spend comes last. (/founder-marketing)
3. Measure brand pull with cheap tests (merch sell-through, list growth, character content) before committing money to shelf-stable. (/founder-brand)
4. Any extension must carry a true "DIRTY" product claim and launch only after greens earn money per container.
5. Financing: co-packer terms or debt secured on orders, sized to the model in condition 1, not a fresh $1M equity raise. (/founder-cfo)
